# Keynote / Conference Tone

Use this tone for keynote talks, conference presentations, pitch decks, and any persuasion-driven deck where the audience attends voluntarily and expects narrative force. NOT for routine briefings (`business.md`), NOT for course lectures (`academic.md`).

## Voice and register

- **Narrative-driven, not information-driven.** A keynote is a story; the slides are punctuation. Story arcs (problem-then-resolution, before-and-after, journey, contrast) carry the deck more than the content does.
- **Conversational, not formal.** Contractions ("we're", "don't") are normal. First-person singular when it's your story. Direct address ("you've all seen this") works.
- **Concrete > abstract, dramatically more than in other formats.** Names of people. Specific dates. The exact words someone said. Stories about a single user, customer, patient — not "the industry".
- **One big idea, repeated.** Keynotes succeed by saying the same thing three different ways, not by saying three things.

## What the deck should NEVER do

- **Information-density slides.** A keynote slide should be readable in 3 seconds while the speaker is mid-sentence. Charts with five series, tables with eight rows, paragraphs of text — all wrong format.
- **Bullets.** Keynote slides use single phrases, single images, single big numbers. Bullets signal "this is information"; keynotes are narrative.
- **Self-reading slides.** The slide should never make the speaker redundant. If the audience can read the slide and get the point, the speaker has nothing to add.
- **Citations on slides.** Save credit for speaker notes or the post-talk handout. Inline citations break the story flow.

## Structure

The dominant skeleton is the **hero's journey / contrast arc**:

1. **Hook** — a story, a question, a contradiction, a striking image. (1–3 slides)
2. **Set up the contrast** — "here's how the world looks today" vs "here's what's possible". (3–5 slides)
3. **The journey** — how you / your team / the field got from one to the other. (5–10 slides)
4. **The payoff** — the take-home, repeated for emphasis. (2–3 slides)
5. **Call to action** — what the audience does next. (1 slide)

Duarte's "sparkline" — every slide categorisable as "today" or "tomorrow", oscillating between them — is the most-validated keynote structure.

## Pacing

- **1.0–2.0 slides/min**. A 20-minute TED-style talk lands 30–60 slides; an 18-minute keynote can lean toward the upper bound.
- Slide changes are punctuation, not information delivery. Many slides are just images or single phrases.

## Worked examples — call them stories

Named individuals. Specific moments. Verbatim quotes. *"Maria, an ICU nurse in Phoenix, told us…"*. The named-person grounding is the keynote's most-validated lever for memorability.

## Active engagement

Direct address, rhetorical questions, predict-then-reveal. Avoid clicker votes / polls unless the venue is small — keynotes are typically one-way.
